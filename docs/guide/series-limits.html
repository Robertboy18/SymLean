      Series and limits

      Taylor and Laurent expansions with a little-o remainder, and limits at a point, from one side or at ±∞.

      On paper a Taylor expansion ends with +…, and I rarely stop to say what the dots mean. Here they become a theorem. Use a series to describe the local expansion of a function, and a limit to prove its limiting value. A series has exact coefficients and a theorem that the remainder is little-o of the last power, in Mathlib’s =o[𝓝 a]. A limit comes with a theorem about Filter.Tendsto on the filter that fits: the neighbourhood of the point or the punctured one, one side of it, or atTop and atBot.

      Series

      • seriesA Taylor or Laurent expansion at a rational point, with exact coefficients and a little-o remainder theorem.

      Limits

      • limitA limit at a rational point, from one side, or at ±∞, with a theorem about Mathlib’s Filter.Tendsto.

      The exponential at 0. The theorem is the precise form of the dots: ex minus the printed polynomial is little-o of x6 as x→0:

      In[1]:=
      #cas [series x 0 6] (exp x)
      Out[1]=

      Taylor polynomial with exact coefficients

      1+x+12x2+16x3+124x4+1120x5+1720x6+o(x6)
      1 + x + 1/2*x^2 + 1/6*x^3 + 1/24*x^4 + 1/120*x^5 + 1/720*x^6 + o(x^6)
      Domain
      x → 0

      The classical limit. In Lean sinx/x is 0/0=0 at x=0, so the value there is beside the point, and the theorem is on the punctured neighbourhood of 0. In a proof, the tactic cas proves the same Tendsto statement:

      In[2]:=
      #cas [limit x → 0] (sin x / x)
      Out[2]=

      exact limit

      1
      Domain
      x → 0, x ≠ 0

      ✓ Checked by Lean’s kernel: Limit from the leading terms of order 1

      Filter.Tendsto (fun x ↦ Real.sin x / x) (nhdsWithin 0 {0}ᶜ) (nhds 1)

      The computation

      • Leading terms

        Numerator 1 (x - a)^1, denominator 1 (x - a)^1 (not proof)

      A series needs exact coefficients, so every function in it has to sit at a point where its value is rational: sin, cos, exp, atan and asin at 0, log and acos at 1, and square roots of rational squares. The same goes for the derivatives that a limit of a quotient uses when its denominator vanishes. The centre of a series is a rational number, and so is the point of a limit, unless it is ±∞. A series goes up to order 12. Some limits at infinity are out of reach of both routes, and then the message says why each one stopped. The tactic cas doesn’t prove the little-o statement, so in a proof I take the printed theorem as a hypothesis.
